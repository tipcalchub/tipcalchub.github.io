How the tip calculator works

The maths behind tipping is simple, but doing it in your head while a server waits with the card machine is another matter. This calculator follows the same steps a careful diner would take on paper. First it takes your bill amount. Then it works out the tax, if you enter a tax rate — handy in US states and cities where sales tax is added on top of menu prices. Next it calculates the tip as a percentage of the bill. By default the tip is figured on the pre-tax subtotal, which is the convention most etiquette guides recommend; tick the box if you prefer to tip on the full amount. Finally it adds everything together and divides the total evenly by the number of people splitting the bill.

Every figure updates the moment you type, so you can experiment freely: nudge the percentage up for great service, add a person to the split, or test how much difference the tax rate makes. Tipping etiquette around the world

The American habit of tipping 15–20% is not a global rule — it is a local custom that can look generous, normal or even rude depending on where you land. In much of Europe a service charge is already folded into the bill, and rounding up or leaving a few coins is plenty. In Japan, tipping is not expected at all and can cause genuine confusion, because excellent service is considered part of the job rather than something bought separately. Australia and New Zealand sit somewhere in between: tipping is appreciated but never demanded, and nobody's wages depend on it.

Before a trip, it pays to read up on the norms for your destination. Our article on tipping around the world covers more than twenty countries, what to leave, and the situations where you should keep your wallet closed. Worked examples

Numbers make the rules easier to remember, so here are three everyday situations run through the calculator above.

Dinner for two. The bill is $100 and the service was good, so you choose 15%. The tip is $15.00, the total is $115.00, and split between two people that is $57.50 each. Try it: type 100 into the calculator, tap 15%, and set the split to 2.

Family meal with tax. A bill of $86.40 in a city with 8.25% sales tax comes to $7.13 in tax. A 20% tip on the pre-tax subtotal is $17.28, making a grand total of $110.81 — or $27.70 each when four people split it evenly.

Group night out. Six friends run up a $240 bar tab. At 20% the tip is $48, the total is $288, and the per-person share lands on a satisfyingly round $48 each. Splitting evenly is exactly where a calculator earns its keep — nobody has to be the table's accountant.

Why tipping exists at all

Modern tipping in the United States grew out of a specific history: after the Civil War, some employers — railways and restaurants among them — hired formerly enslaved people and paid them little or nothing, expecting customers' tips to make up the wage. The practice stuck, and today the US federal tipped minimum wage is still far below the standard minimum wage, which is why tips in America are closer to a wage contribution than a bonus. In most of Europe, by contrast, service staff are paid a full wage and a service charge is often included by law or custom, so tips there really are a small extra thank-you. Understanding that difference explains most of the confusing variation you will meet — and it is why the same 20% that is ordinary in Chicago would be startling in Copenhagen. How Much to Tip: The Complete Guide

A practical guide to standard tip amounts for everyday services.

Few small social questions cause as much quiet anxiety as "how much should I tip?" Tip too little and you feel stingy; tip too much and you have overpaid for a haircut. The good news is that there are settled norms for almost every situation. This guide collects them in one place, explains why they are what they are, and flags the cases where tipping is truly optional. When you know the amount, use the tip calculator to split it fairly.

Restaurants and bars

For table service in the United States, 15% of the pre-tax bill is the accepted floor for adequate service, 18–20% is the standard for good service, and 25% or more is a genuine compliment. These percentages are high by world standards because US servers are often paid a reduced "tipped wage" by their employer, with tips expected to make up the difference. At a bar, the convention is simpler: $1–2 per drink, or 15–20% if you are settling a tab at the end of the night. For a deeper look at the restaurant rules — large parties, wine, split bills — read our dedicated guide to tipping in restaurants.

Delivery and takeaway

Food delivery drivers should receive 15–20% of the order, with a sensible minimum of $3–5 even on small orders, because the driver's effort barely changes with the size of the bag. Add more for long distances, large orders, stairs, or bad weather. Grocery and alcohol delivery follow the same pattern. Takeaway is different: when you collect food from a counter, a tip is a kind gesture rather than an obligation — 10% or the spare change is generous, and leaving nothing is socially acceptable, whatever the payment screen suggests.

Hotels

Hotels involve several different people, each tipped separately and usually in cash. Housekeeping: $2–5 per night, left daily rather than in a lump sum at checkout, because a different person may clean your room each day. Bell staff: $1–2 per bag. Doorman: $1–2 for hailing a taxi or helping with luggage. Concierge: $5–20 depending on the difficulty of the request — a restaurant recommendation costs nothing, but scoring sold-out theatre tickets deserves the top of the range. Room service usually has a service charge built in; check before adding more, as explained in our article on tips vs. service charges.

Personal services

Hairdressers and barbers receive 15–20% of the service price. If an assistant washed your hair, a separate $2–5 is a thoughtful touch. Nail technicians, massage therapists and spa staff sit in the same 15–20% band. Tattoo artists are commonly tipped 15–25%, reflecting the permanence of the work. Movers — often forgotten — deserve $20–40 per person for a half day, more for stairs, pianos and summer heat.

Travel and transport

Taxi and rideshare drivers: 15–20% of the fare, rounding up on short trips. Airport shuttle drivers: $2–5 per person or per bag handled. Tour guides: 10–20% of the tour price, or the per-person amount suggested by the operator on group tours. Valet attendants are tipped $2–5 when your car is returned — that timing matters, because the tip should reach the person who actually fetched it.

When tipping is optional — or inappropriate

Not every outstretched hand expects a tip. Counter staff at coffee shops and fast-food restaurants are paid full wages, so the tip jar is genuinely optional. Professionals such as plumbers, electricians and teachers are not tipped in most cultures — a good review or a repeat booking is the appropriate thank-you. And in some countries, notably Japan, offering a tip can create an awkward moment rather than a warm one; our guide to tipping around the world covers where to keep your wallet closed.

A simple rule of thumb

If you remember nothing else: 15% for adequate, 20% for good, cash where possible for hotel staff, and always check the bill first for charges already added. Tipping well is cheap goodwill — the difference between 15% and 20% on a $60 dinner is three dollars, and it is remembered long after the meal is forgotten.

Tipping in Restaurants: Everything Diners Get Wrong

The rules, the grey areas, and the mistakes that cost diners money.

Restaurant tipping seems simple — a percentage at the end of the meal — yet it generates more confusion than every other kind of tipping combined. Do you tip on the total including tax? What if the restaurant added a charge already? Does a big group change the rules? Here are the questions diners actually argue about, answered plainly.

Tip on the pre-tax amount

The standard is to calculate your tip on the bill before sales tax is added. Tax is a government levy, not a service the restaurant provided, and tipping on it quietly inflates your gratuity — in a high-tax city, tipping 20% on the post-tax total is effectively tipping 22%. The TipCalcHub calculator follows the pre-tax convention by default and lets you switch it on if you prefer to be generous. Either way, do it deliberately rather than by accident.

Check for charges already added

Before you write in a tip, read the bill. Many restaurants add an automatic gratuity — often 18–20% — for parties of six or more, and a growing number add a small "kitchen appreciation" or service fee for all tables. If a full gratuity is already there, adding another 20% on top means tipping 40%, which flatters nobody's budget. The line between a tip and a service charge matters here, because the two are treated differently by law; our explainer on tip vs. service charge shows you how to tell them apart on a real bill.

Your server does not keep the whole tip

Many diners imagine their tip lands entirely in their server's pocket. In reality, most restaurants operate a tip pool or "tip-out": your server shares a percentage with bartenders, bussers, food runners and sometimes hosts. That is one reason tipping on the full bill — including drinks — is expected even if a bartender poured them. It also means a harsh tip for a kitchen mistake punishes the wrong people; if the food was the problem, mention it to a manager rather than docking the server.

What about genuinely bad service?

The accepted approach is to tip for the service you received, but to speak up before you settle the bill. A cold dish, a forgotten order or a long wait is usually fixable in the moment, and managers would rather hear it from you than read it in a review. If the service was truly neglectful, reducing the tip to 10% sends a clear signal; leaving nothing at all is widely read as "I forgot" rather than "I was unhappy", so if you do it, say why — politely, to the manager, not as a parting shot.

Splitting the bill fairly

Splitting evenly is painless when everyone ordered similarly, and quietly unfair when one person had water and a salad while another had cocktails and steak. Two honest methods work well: split evenly when the orders are roughly comparable (it keeps friendships smooth), or ask the server for separate checks at the start of the meal — never at the end. When splitting evenly, run the total through the calculator with the number of people entered, and each person's share including their portion of the tip appears instantly.

Cash or card?

Servers generally prefer cash: it reaches them the same night, untouched by card-processing fees that some restaurants deduct from card tips. Card tips are still perfectly acceptable and are increasingly the only option at cashless restaurants. What matters most is that the tip is proportionate and the bill is checked. Tip vs. Service Charge: What Is the Difference?

They look the same on the bill. Legally and practically, they are not.

Two lines on a restaurant bill can look identical — "gratuity 18%" and "service charge 18%" — yet they mean different things, go to different people, and leave you with different obligations. Understanding the distinction takes two minutes and can save you from double-tipping or, worse, under-tipping staff who received nothing.

What a tip is

A tip (or gratuity) is a voluntary payment from a customer to an employee. The key word is voluntary: the amount is your choice, and under US labour rules the money belongs to the employees, not the business. Employers may organise a tip pool among staff who customarily receive tips, but they cannot keep tip money for the house or use it to pay managers. When you add a tip on a card slip or in cash, you can be reasonably confident it reaches the staff.

What a service charge is

A service charge is a mandatory fee set by the business. Because it is compulsory, it is legally treated as revenue of the business — like the price of the food. The restaurant may pass some, all or none of it to staff, and it is not required to tell you the split unless local law says otherwise (a few US cities and states now mandate disclosure). Service charges are common for large parties, banquets, room service and catering contracts, and they are the default model in much of Europe, where "service compris" means service is included in menu prices.

Why the difference matters to you

Three practical consequences follow. First, double-tipping: if a mandatory 18% service charge or automatic gratuity is already on the bill, adding a standard tip on top is usually unnecessary — a small extra for exceptional service is a choice, not an expectation. Second, staff income: a generous-looking service charge may not reach your server at all, which is why some diners add a modest cash tip even when a charge is present. Third, tax: in many jurisdictions a mandatory service charge is taxed like a sale, while a voluntary tip is not, which slightly changes the final total you will see in the calculator.

How to read your bill

  • "Gratuity" or "tip" with a blank line beside it — voluntary; nothing has been added yet.
  • "Gratuity included" or "automatic gratuity" — already added, usually for large parties. Adding more is optional.
  • "Service charge" — mandatory business fee. Ask, if you like, how much reaches the staff; good restaurants answer happily.
  • "Kitchen fee", "wellness charge", "administrative fee" — business fees, not tips. They do not replace a tip for your server.

If anything is unclear, asking "is any of this a tip that goes to the staff?" is a normal, welcome question — staff would rather you ask than guess wrong in either direction.

The short version

A tip is your money given to staff by choice. A service charge is the business's money, charged by rule. When a full service charge is already applied, a further tip is a bonus, not a duty; when only small fees appear, your server is still relying on a normal tip. Tipping Around the World: A Traveller's Guide

What to leave — and what to keep in your pocket — in more than twenty countries.

Tipping is one of the easiest ways to accidentally break a social rule abroad. The percentage that marks you as fair in New York marks you as flashy in Paris and as confusing in Tokyo. The differences are not random: they track how service staff are paid in each country. Where wages are low and tipping is built into the pay system, tips are large and expected. Where staff earn a full wage and service is priced in, tips shrink to a token — or disappear entirely.

Where tipping is expected and substantial

United States and Canada lead the world: 15–20% in restaurants, $1–2 per drink at bars, and tips for everyone from taxi drivers to hotel housekeepers. Mexico follows at 10–15% in restaurants, with small cash tips for hotel staff and petrol station attendants. In much of the Middle East — Egypt, Jordan, the UAE — 10–15% is customary in restaurants, often on top of a service charge already in the bill, and small notes for hotel staff are routine. South Africa expects 10–15% in restaurants and small amounts for car guards and petrol attendants.

Where service is included and small tips suffice

In France, law requires menu prices to include service ("service compris"), so the bill is the bill; leaving a few euros in cash for good service is a kind extra, not a duty. Italy adds a "coperto" cover charge, and rounding up or leaving €1–2 per person is plenty. Spain and Portugal are similar: locals leave small change or round up, and 5–10% marks you as generous. In Germany and Austria, tipping is real but modest — round the bill up to a round number and hand it to the server directly, saying the total you want to pay, rather than leaving cash on the table. The United Kingdom sits at 10–12.5% in restaurants, frequently added as an optional service charge you may ask to remove; pub staff are not tipped per drink, though "and one for yourself" remains a charming offer some bartenders accept as a small cash amount.

Where tipping is not the custom

Japan is the famous case: there is no tipping culture, staff are paid full wages, and an offered tip may be politely refused or chased after you as forgotten money. Excellent service is simply the standard. South Korea and China are similar in everyday restaurants and taxis, though high-end hotels in China increasingly accept tips for specific help. In Australia and New Zealand, tipping is never required — wages are comparatively high — but leaving 10% for genuinely good restaurant service is common and appreciated. Scandinavia prices service in; rounding up is a pleasant gesture, no more.

Universal traveller's rules

  • Check the bill first: a service charge may already be included, as explained in tip vs. service charge.
  • Tip in local currency, in cash, directly to the person where customs allow — it is the surest way the money arrives.
  • When in doubt in a new country, 10% in a restaurant will rarely offend anyone outside the no-tipping cultures listed above.
  • Ask your hotel concierge or a local colleague; tipping norms are a question everyone understands.

Frequently Asked Questions

Straight answers to the tipping questions people ask most. For the full percentage tables, see how much to tip: the complete guide.

How do I calculate a tip quickly?

Move the decimal point: 10% of the bill is the bill divided by ten. Double that for 20%, and add half again for 15%. Or skip the mental maths and use the calculator on our homepage — it also handles tax and splitting.

Should the tip be calculated before or after tax?

Before tax is the standard recommendation, because tax is not part of the service. Our calculator uses the pre-tax amount by default, with an option to include tax if you prefer.

What is a good tip for food delivery?

15–20% of the order, with a $3–5 minimum. Add more for distance, large orders, stairs or bad weather — the driver's effort is mostly fixed, whatever the order costs.

Do I tip if a service charge is already on the bill?

Usually you do not need to add a full tip on top. A service charge is a mandatory business fee and may not reach the staff; a small extra in cash is a kind choice for excellent service. Our tip vs. service charge explainer covers the details.

How much should I tip the hairdresser?

15–20% of the service price, plus $2–5 for an assistant who washed your hair, if applicable.

Is it better to tip in cash or by card?

Cash reaches staff fastest and in full, and is preferred for hotel housekeeping. Card tips are fine and sometimes the only option. Either is far better than debating it and tipping nothing.

What if the service was bad?

Raise the problem during the meal, when it can still be fixed. If it stays poor, reducing the tip to around 10% is a recognised signal; leaving nothing is usually misread as forgetfulness.

Do I tip on takeaway orders?

It is optional. Counter staff are paid full wages, so anything you leave — 10% or loose change — is a bonus, not an obligation.

How does bill splitting work with a tip?

Add the tip and any tax to the bill first, then divide the grand total by the number of people. The calculator does this automatically and shows each person's share and their portion of the tip.
